How FamilyOS touches the web, and what stays private

Four ways FamilyOS reaches a website, two ways a phone hands a notice in, and the encryption under all of it.

Never holds a card, a UPI PIN or a bank login, and never paysA person approves every date, task and errandEvery read, write and run is in the audit

1. Reading the public web with no credential

A fee schedule, a public booking page or a URL someone shared is read once, on request, never signed in. The reader (execution/disposable.py) fetches the page with plain HTTP and receives no credential of any kind: no cookie jar, no token, no household key. It cannot authenticate even if a page tries to talk it into it, so a page that argues is a bad claim rather than a leak.

JavaScript pages that hand a plain fetch an empty shell go through a research provider instead (research.py, Parallel, off unless configured). The query is built from a plan option's own words and scrubbed of every member's name before it leaves. The reader announces itself in its user agent.

What comes back is evidence, never fact: the URL it landed on, the page hashed, the time it was read. It is shown with its source and never becomes a claim or an obligation on its own.

2. Acting on a site the family is signed in to

An errand runs only after a guardian approves the exact request by its fingerprint. The sign-in gets in through a sitting: a watched browser on the sign-in page, where the person signs in themselves. FamilyOS never sees the password and never re-authenticates on its own. At the end of the sitting only that site's origins are read out and sealed under the household key as a sealed session; the seal binds the household and the service into the ciphertext.

Whatever decides what to do holds a capability: a name, a service and the origins it may reach, with no cookie or token in it. Only the driver, the code that steers the browser, is lent the session, and only for one task. The browser for that task is thrown away afterwards.

The phone has a fourth pair of hands for apps with no web page at all: a test runner on a paired phone reads the school's app through its accessibility tree and hands the words in as a private notice. When a run needs a person, the console's live view is relayed by the server frame for frame, nothing read or kept on the way, and since 10 Oct the asker is told at once on Telegram with the link to take over.

Every browser appends one token to its real user agent, FamilyOS/0.2 (+household agent; each run approved by a guardian; never pays), never in place of it, so the string tells one story and a site can tell a household's agent from a person. Signed requests (Web Bot Auth, RFC 9421) need a stable HTTPS origin for the key directory; a domain in front of a static host or Tailscale Funnel gives that.

A runbook never lets a model read the page; it may answer one closed question over a short list the code extracted. The generalist, the fallback for a site with no runbook, is the exception: the model sees the page through Anthropic's browser toolset, inside the site's own hosts and a confirmation gate that refuses any click that would pay, order or confirm. It is skipped when the household runs local models.

3. The share sheet and Shortcuts

Most of a family's notices live where no API reaches: a WhatsApp group, a school app, a screenshot, a forwarded PDF. The share sheet is the one door every app on the phone already has, which is why those notices reach FamilyOS at all.

  • The share extension. From any app's share sheet, "Send to FamilyOS" takes the photo, PDF, page or text, lets the person tag a child and choose private or shared, and posts the raw bytes with the app's device token. Offline, it waits in an outbox.
  • The Shortcut. A phone without the app does the same request from a Shortcut with a device token pasted once. On iOS 27 the Notification trigger can run it when a named app posts an alert, so a school app's notice shares itself.
  • App Intents. "Ask FamilyOS", "What's on today" and "Share with FamilyOS" as Siri phrases and Shortcuts actions.

The server treats a share as the plainest rail: the phone says nothing about where it came from, so the bytes are private to the member who shared them unless they chose shared; duplicates are dropped by hash; a child tag is accepted only when a guardian has recorded consent; a bare URL is read once as a public page. Everything is sealed before it is stored and read by the same reader as mail. A device token is shown once, never expires on its own, and is revocable from the Household card without signing the app out.

4. Encryption, and what it covers

Originals are sealed per household; derived facts rely on database controls. That is the one-line statement, and the rest is detail.

Keys. A master key in the environment wraps each household's 256-bit key, which wraps a key per blob. Originals are sealed with AES-GCM before they reach the object store, under opaque ids rather than filenames or hashes, so the store learns nothing about the content.

Sealed under the household keyPlain rows, by design
Every original: mail bodies, PDFs, images, harvested page text. The Gmail refresh token. Browser session state from a sitting. Biller consumer numbers are not stored at all, only an HMAC. Session tokens, sign-in links, device tokens and founding tickets are hashed.Claims, obligations, who a notice is about, the sender and subject, members and households. The brief, the inbox and Money are queries over them and could not sort or group over ciphertext. They rely on disk encryption, one database role per deployment, no shared credentials and an append-only audit.

Erasure. Destroying a household's wrapped key is the first step: from that moment every copy of its originals, in backups or a lagging replica, and every sealed session, is unreadable. Withdrawing a child's consent erases what names them in the same transaction.

Projection, not twin. Every fact on a screen is derived from a notice and points back to it. There is no separate memory to leak, drift or outlive an erasure.
